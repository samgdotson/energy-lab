# Midwest24k, August 2015: resource maps, line loading, and siting a 500 MW wind farm and a 200 MW / 4 h battery

*Written 2026-10-31 from the solved August 2015 DC-OPF (31 days, 744 hours, 23,643 buses). The
figures and tables come from three Snakemake rules: `resource_maps`, `line_loading` and `siting`.
Settings are in `config/siting.yaml`. The narrative and judgements are the author's. Background on
the base run is in `LOGBOOK.md` §§6–7.*

---

## 1. Summary

**What the model says about prices.**
- After removing the congestion term of one suspect line (see below), the spatial price signal
  in August 2015 is weak. The system-wide energy price averages **$34.46/MWh**, and almost
  every bus is within ±$1 of it.
- One line accounts for most of the spatial differences: **L4698**, a 359 MVA, 138 kV line
  between the Braidwood nuclear plant and Kankakee, IL. Its congestion price averages
  **$371/MW**, and through the network it adds +$4 to +$6/MWh in IN, MI and KY and takes $2–3/MWh
  off WI, MN and IA.
- LOGBOOK F12 already flagged that line as a probable data error. Re-solving two days with it
  uprated shows that **part** of the east–west difference is real: other 345 kV constraints
  around Chicago and into Michigan start binding (§4).

**Wind (500 MW).** The recommended sites are the best wind resource with spare 230–345 kV
capacity, plus one site near load in Michigan. None needs curtailment for grid limits in the
screen.

**The most important caveat for an investor.** These are **one-month energy-only values from a
linear model with no scarcity pricing**.
- The best battery earns about **$0.26/kW-month** in arbitrage, more than an order of magnitude
  below what a 4-hour battery needs to recover its cost.
- The 500 MW wind farm earns about **$3.3 M/month** (about $38/MWh at W1).
- The ranking is informative; the absolute values are not bankable. §7 explains why.

## 3. What sets prices: a decomposition

In a lossless DC OPF, every bus price is the system energy price plus one term per binding
branch: λ_t − Σ_k μ_k,t · PTDF_k,i. The `siting` rule fits this exactly, with a maximum
residual of 1.7e-7 $/MWh, using PTDF rows for the 14 branches that ever bind. That gives the
hourly congestion price μ of each branch and its contribution to every bus.

![L4698 price effect](../results/2015-08/siting/artefact_price_effect.png)

| State | Mean LMP | Energy | L4698 term | All other congestion |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| IN | 40.46 | 34.46 | **+5.93** | +0.07 |
| MI | 38.90 | 34.46 | **+4.21** | +0.24 |
| KY | 38.49 | 34.46 | **+4.01** | +0.01 |
| IL | 32.78 | 34.46 | −1.67 | −0.02 |
| NE | 33.06 | 34.46 | −1.34 | −0.07 |
| MN | 31.97 | 34.46 | −2.43 | −0.05 |
| WI | 31.05 | 34.46 | −3.37 | −0.05 |
| NM | 33.29 | 34.46 | −0.65 | −0.51 |

- **L4698 dominates.** Its congestion price averages **$371/MW**. The next largest are L5975
  ($11.5), T5529 ($3.8), T7968 ($3.2) and L19001 ($2.3); the rest are below $1.
- **Local extremes around Braidwood.** At the Braidwood bus, L4698 congestion subtracts
  $93/MWh, so the bus averages −$59. At Kankakee it adds about $50/MWh.
- **The model's own suspects are this line's neighbours.** "Hot spots" at Dresden, Elwood,
  Joliet, Pilot Hill, Kelly Creek, Hoopeston and Danville all inherit $10–35/MWh from it.

## 4. Is the L4698 effect real? Two validation re-solves

We can't tell from the data alone whether L4698's 359 MVA rating is wrong, and that matters for
siting. So Aug 14 (peak load) and Aug 22 (windy, low price) were re-solved with L4698 uprated to
1,500 MVA, everything else unchanged. These were one-off runs; their outputs are in the session
scratchpad, not the workflow.

| | Aug 14 base | Aug 14 uprated | Aug 22 base | Aug 22 uprated |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Objective ($M) | 39.59 | 39.03 (−1.4%) | 10.57 | 10.00 (−5.4%) |
| Braidwood nuclear output (GWh) | 52.8 | 58.8 | **13.7** | **58.4** |
| Bus-mean LMP range, 2nd–98th pct. ($/MWh) | 31.0–44.1 | 35.5–40.7 | 21.7–33.5 | 21.4–33.4 |
| MI / WI mean LMP ($/MWh) | 41.6 / 33.3 | 39.8 / 36.2 | 32.0 / 24.4 | 31.8 / 23.3 |
| Newly binding branches | | L2849, L4005, L5863, L5952 (345/138 kV corridor) | | L4005, L5975 |

**Findings.**
1. **Fixing L4698 frees Braidwood.** On the windy day the plant's output quadruples. It is
   clearly a binding outlet constraint, worth $0.5–0.6 M a day in production cost.
2. **The east–west price difference shrinks but does not vanish.** Once L4698 is relieved,
   power from Braidwood and the west reaches the same Chicago → Michigan/Indiana corridor (§2.2)
   and binds other 345 kV lines instead.
   - On the peak day the MI premium over WI falls from $8.4 to $3.6/MWh.
   - On the windy day it doesn't shrink at all: $7.6/MWh in the base case, $8.5 uprated.
3. **Neither raw nor first-order-adjusted prices are right, but raw prices have the better
   spatial pattern.**

   | | Mean abs. error vs re-solve, Aug 14 / Aug 22 | Correlation of bus means with re-solve |
   |---|---|---|
   | Raw | $2.07 / $1.54 | 0.73 / 0.89 |
   | Adjusted (L4698 term removed) | $0.97 / $2.63 | 0.50 / 0.70 |

   Siting depends on the spatial pattern, so **the screen ranks on raw prices** and reports
   adjusted-price values as a sensitivity.
   - A site is **excluded** when L4698 moves its mean price by more than $5/MWh. Errors grow
     with the size of the L4698 term: $0.5–1.9 mean error below $2/MWh, $0.9–3.1 for
     $2–5/MWh, and $1.8–7.0 above $5/MWh. The $5 cut-off is a judgement call along that
     gradient, not a sharp break.
   - That excludes the Braidwood/Kankakee/Joliet/Danville pocket but keeps Michigan.
4. **L5975 (Elwood) is sensitive to the fix.** It went from 13 h to 0 h at its limit on Aug 14,
   and from 0 h to 17 h on Aug 22. Anything that depends on it (B1) carries extra uncertainty.

The proper fix is to resolve L4698's rating (check against the source case and the PowerWorld
dispatch), then **re-solve the whole month**. That takes about 4.5 h with two days at a time.
After that the siting rule can rerun unchanged.

## 5. Screening method

**Candidates.**
- Wind: all 1,841 buses at **≥ 230 kV**, the minimum sensible for a 500 MW interconnection.
- Batteries: all 12,009 buses at **≥ 115 kV**.

**Wind screen.**
1. **Hourly output.** Capacity factor at each candidate's ERA5 cell, using the turbine model
   from dispatch.
2. **Market value.** Σ_t CF_t · LMP_t, the energy a price-taker would sell at its own node.
   - The **capture price** is value per MWh.
   - The **value factor** is capture price ÷ mean nodal price. Below 1, the wind blows when
     prices are low.
3. **Deliverability** for the top 200 by value.
   - Compute the PTDF column for injecting at the bus and withdrawing across all load.
   - Each hour, the **hosting capacity** is H_t = min over branches with |PTDF| ≥ 5% of
     (rating − flow in the injection direction) / |PTDF|. The 5% cut-off is the same idea as
     the distribution-factor thresholds in interconnection screening studies.
   - Output above H_t counts as curtailed, and the value counts only deliverable energy.
4. **Congestion side-effects.** The extra flow the plant puts on already-binding branch-hours,
   valued at that branch's hourly μ.
5. **Context.** Rating and mean loading of the branches at the bus, PTDF-weighted loading of
   its footprint, load within 150 km, existing wind within 50 km.

**Battery screen.**
1. **Fast screen.** For every candidate, the average daily spread between the 4 highest and 4
   lowest prices, after losses and degradation. Buses with identical price series are merged,
   which leaves 8,929 distinct series.
2. **Arbitrage LP** for the 400 best distinct series, over the whole month with perfect
   foresight:
   - 200 MW / 800 MWh, 85% round trip, at most one full cycle a day;
   - $5/MWh degradation cost on discharge;
   - charge + discharge ≤ 200 MW;
   - end state of charge ≥ start;
   - power bounded **hour by hour by the hosting capacity** for discharging (injection) and
     charging (withdrawal).
   - The LP is solved three ways: with grid limits, without them, and on the alternative price
     basis.
3. **Congestion relief.** MWh by which the battery's schedule pushes binding branches back from
   their limits, valued at the hourly μ. This is the system benefit a storage-as-transmission
   argument would point to.

**Selection.**
- Rank by value. Skip flagged sites (L4698 effect > $5/MWh).
- Keep sites at least 120 km apart and at most 2 per state, so the five picks cover different
  situations rather than one cluster.

**Everything is in CSVs.**
- `results/2015-08/siting/wind_screen.csv` and `battery_screen.csv` hold all shortlisted
  candidates with every metric.
- `wind_sites.csv` and `battery_sites.csv` hold the picks.
- `lmp_decomposition_by_bus.csv` and `congestion_prices_hourly.csv` hold the decomposition.

## 6. The proposed sites in detail

### 6.1 Wind farm, 500 MW

![Wind value](../results/2015-08/siting/wind_value.png)

Across all wind candidates, value tracks the resource. After the L4698 artefact the price
differences are small, so a site with a capacity factor 0.04 higher beats most price advantages.
The exception is Michigan, where a real import constraint (L11516, plus whatever replaces L4698)
adds a few dollars per MWh. The first four picks were best overall; W5 comes in through the
diversity rules.

**W1 – Ludington, MI (bus 181756, 345 kV). The near-load option.**
- **Resource.** Lakeshore wind on the eastern shore of Lake Michigan: Aug CF 0.238, the best
  onshore resource in the Lower Peninsula.
- **Price.**
  - The capture price is **$38.49/MWh**, $5–8 above the Plains sites, and the value factor is
    0.97 (output is spread evenly across the day).
  - That gives the highest market value in the screen, **$6.82k/MW-month**, about $3.4 M/month
    for 500 MW.
- **Grid.**
  - Ludington is a major 345 kV hub, home of the real Ludington pumped-storage plant, with
    **6.3 GVA** of branch capacity at the bus.
  - Local branches are moderately loaded (31%). Hosting capacity is at least 666 MW in 95% of
    hours, so no curtailment is needed.
- **Where the power goes.**
  - 1.9 GW of load lies within 150 km, and the plant injects *inside* the Michigan import
    area. That relieves the binding Sturgis–Battle Creek 345 kV line (L11516) and the Mackinac
    line (L13281).
  - It has the largest congestion relief of any wind site, about **$35.5k/month**, and adds
    almost nothing elsewhere.
- **Risks.**
  - The price premium partly depends on L4698: on adjusted prices the value drops to $6.02k,
    well below the Plains sites. The validation re-solves kept part of the MI premium (all of it on the windy day) (§4), so the premium is
    real but smaller than the raw numbers suggest.
  - Lakeshore siting constraints (dunes, viewsheds, permitting).
  - The ERA5 cells along the shore mix land and water, so the true onshore capacity factor may
    be lower.

**W2 – Valentine, NE (bus 240319, 345 kV). The best resource on a strong backbone.**
- **Resource.** Aug CF **0.282**, among the best anywhere in the footprint, in the Sandhills of
  north-central Nebraska.
- **Price.** Capture price $31.72 (value factor 0.96). Value $6.66k/MW-month raw, and
  **$6.96k, the best of all,** on adjusted prices. That makes it the most robust pick across
  both price bases.
- **Grid.**
  - 345 kV with **3.55 GVA** at the bus and 14% loading.
  - Hosting capacity is ≥ 1,778 MW in 95% of hours, more than three times the plant.
  - The PTDF footprint averages 16% loading.
- **Where the power goes.** There's essentially no local load (0.05 GW within 150 km), so
  every MWh travels the 345 kV system east. The model has room for it: the only effect on
  binding branches is a small push on the Michigan import line L11516, about $10k/month of
  congestion.
- **Risks.**
  - Long-distance delivery makes it exposed to future congestion and to basis risk between its
    node and a trading hub once more wind joins.
  - SPP/MISO seam issues aren't modelled.

**W3 – Lake Benton II, MN (bus 191332, 230 kV). The proven Buffalo Ridge resource.**
- **Resource.** Aug CF **0.286**, the highest of any candidate, on Buffalo Ridge in southwest
  Minnesota, one of the most developed wind areas in the US. There is 1.5 GW of existing wind
  within 50 km.
- **Price.** Capture price $30.46 and a value factor of 0.94; it overlaps in time with the
  existing fleet. Value $6.48k raw / $6.88k adjusted, second on adjusted prices.
- **Grid.**
  - 230 kV with 1.04 GVA at the bus, almost unloaded (1.3%).
  - Hosting capacity ≥ 745 MW in 95% of hours, so 500 MW fits but uses most of the margin.
  - 0.8 GW of load within 150 km (Sioux Falls, Marshall).
- **Risks.**
  - In reality Buffalo Ridge is a well-known congestion and curtailment area, constrained by
    N-1 contingency limits this model doesn't have (§7).
  - The 230 kV outlet would likely need upgrades. Treat the clean deliverability result here
    as optimistic.

**W4 – Atkinson, NE (bus 240198, 345 kV). The most grid headroom.**
- **Resource.** Aug CF 0.275, 80 km east of Valentine.
- **Value.** $6.42k raw / $6.71k adjusted.
- **Grid.** 345 kV with **4.9 GVA** at the bus, 10% loaded. Hosting capacity is ≥ 2,449 MW in
  95% of hours, the largest of any top-ranked site. It could take a much larger project or a
  wind + battery hybrid.
- **Risks.** Same remoteness as W2 (0.2 GW of load within 150 km). Nebraska's public-power
  utilities also add offtake and interconnection steps a merchant developer would need to plan
  for.

**W5 – Watertown, SD (bus 270187, 230 kV). A good resource at a lightly used hub.**
- **Resource.** Aug CF 0.274 in northeast South Dakota.
- **Value.** $6.23k raw / $6.61k adjusted.
- **Grid.** 230 kV with 2.7 GVA at the bus, 8% loaded. Hosting capacity ≥ 964 MW in 95% of
  hours. 0.33 GW of local load.
- **Why it's on the list.** Buffalo Ridge sites ranked higher in Minnesota and South Dakota but
  sit within 120 km of W3. Watertown is the best site that adds geographic diversity, which
  means less correlated output with W3.

**Wind sites considered and rejected.**
- **Northern Michigan (Munising, Detour, Heritage Garden).** Good lakeshore resource, but
  8–11% curtailment because L13281 (Mackinac crossing, 138 kV) limits export to the south.
- **Mission SD, Marshall MN.** 2–3% curtailment from local 115–230 kV limits.
- **Anything near Braidwood/Kankakee.** Flagged: its prices are dominated by L4698.

### 6.2 Battery, 200 MW / 800 MWh

![Battery price duration](../results/2015-08/siting/battery_price_duration.png)

- **There is very little spread in this model.** Most buses see a daily 4-hour spread of $8–11
  and a price range of about $12–50/MWh.
  - Battery value therefore comes from two things: **local congestion pockets** (where binding
    branches make the price swing more than the system price) and **negative prices** at wind
    export pockets.
  - Every site below is chosen for a specific binding constraint it sits behind or in front of.
- **Value of grid limits.** The column "no grid limit" in `battery_sites.csv` shows what the
  battery would earn if it could always charge and discharge at 200 MW. The gap is the value
  lost to the hosting limits.

**B1 – Lincoln Generating Facility, IL (bus 133365, 138 kV). An import pocket at Elwood/Joliet.**
- **The pocket.**
  - The 138 kV system around Elwood and Joliet imports through **L5975** (Joliet refinery →
    Elwood, 305 MVA). L5975 averages 92% loading and is at its limit for **171 h**, when prices
    downstream jump by about $47/MWh.
  - Lincoln sits just downstream. In its top 20% of hours its price is $5–8 above the
    system energy price (blue curve).
- **Value.**
  - Highest arbitrage among eligible sites: **$52.5k/month raw, $47.7k adjusted**, so robust.
  - It cycles most often (16.5 full cycles a month), buying at $30.2 and selling at $44.5.
- **Grid.**
  - Discharging is never constrained (hosting ≥ 991 MW).
  - Charging is blocked in some hours (5th-percentile charge hosting is 0 MW), because charging
    pulls more power through the congested L5975. The LP respects this.
- **Congestion relief.** **1,032 MWh** off binding L5975 branch-hours, worth **$57.7k** at its
  congestion price. That's the largest system benefit of any site: the battery works like a
  small, dispatchable transmission upgrade for the pocket.
- **Risks.**
  - L5975 is next to the L4698 problem area. In the validation re-solves its binding pattern
    changed substantially (§4). L4698 moves Lincoln's mean price by only $1.7, but the pocket
    itself could grow or shrink once the rating is fixed.
  - Elwood (bus 133345) scored even higher ($128–148k) but is excluded: L4698 moves its price
    by $12.
  - Several real peakers sit in this area, and their behaviour, which this model simplifies,
    sets the pocket's prices.

**B2 – Cheboygan, MI (bus 181569, 138 kV). Between two binding Michigan constraints.**
- **The pocket.** The northern Lower Peninsula sits between the Mackinac crossing to the Upper
  Peninsula (**L13281**, 18 h at limit) and the 345 kV path into Michigan from the south
  (**L11516**, 139 h).
- **Value.**
  - The two constraints alternate, which lifts peak prices to $49/MWh, while the lows follow
    the system price down to $11.89 (the nuclear bid).
  - $47.7k/month raw, $38.3k adjusted.
- **Congestion relief.** 756 MWh worth $13.9k a month, mostly on L11516.
- **Grid.**
  - Charging is blocked in some hours: charging adds to the import over L11516/L13281 when
    those are binding.
  - Discharging hosting is ≥ 205 MW, so it can always discharge at full power.
- **Risks.**
  - About $3.9/MWh of its mean price comes from L4698, just under the exclusion line, and the
    value drops 20% on adjusted prices.
  - The 138 kV connection limits any expansion.
  - **Alternative:** Saint Ignace (bus 181606), on the Upper Peninsula side of L13281, is less
    dependent on L4698 ($1.1) and earns $36.7k raw / $34.8k adjusted.

**B3 – Midland, MI (bus 180992, 345 kV). A strong node inside the Michigan import area.**
- **Why here.**
  - Midland is a 345 kV hub (3.8 GVA at the bus, ≥ 1,947 MW discharge hosting) in the middle of
    Michigan, downstream of **L11516**.
  - Its price is consistently high: mean $39.8, max $47.9. It moves with the Michigan import
    constraint and with the bigger Chicago–Michigan corridor that the validation re-solves
    showed persists.
- **Value.** $46.7k/month raw, $34.4k adjusted.
- **Congestion relief.** 935 MWh, $8.5k.
- **Compared with B2.** Midland is the more scalable site: a 345 kV connection could take
  400–800 MW of storage. Its value is mostly the Michigan price *level* rather than local
  volatility, so it's more exposed to whether the MI premium survives the L4698 fix. L4698 adds
  $5.0/MWh here, right at the threshold.

**B4 – Mahomet, IL (bus 130189, 345 kV). Downstream of the Clinton nuclear outlet.**
- **The constraint.**
  - Clinton Power Station's 345 kV outlet to Farmer City (**L5863**, 1,306 MVA) averages 86%
    loading and binds 15 h in the base case.
  - In the uprated re-solve of Aug 14 it bound in **15 h of that one day** (0 h in the base).
    Fixing L4698 makes this constraint *more* important.
- **Value.** Mahomet, near Champaign, sits on the far side of it: $44.5k/month raw, $35.2k
  adjusted.
- **Congestion relief.** Discharging pushes against Clinton's export, relieving L5863 by 476
  MWh ($14.9k).
- **Grid.** 345 kV with 5.2 GVA at the bus, discharge hosting ≥ 1,031 MW, charge hosting ≥ 121
  MW.
- **Alternative.** A battery at the **Clinton Power Station** bus (132709) itself would charge
  when the outlet is congested. It is less exposed to L4698 ($1.5): $42.6k raw / $39.4k
  adjusted, the most robust value across both bases after Lincoln.

**B5 – Bison 4 Wind Energy Center, ND (bus 230269, 115 kV). Storing curtailed wind.**
- **The pocket.**
  - The Bison wind cluster exports through 115 kV transformers and lines (**T7449**, L18196,
    T7450). When it's windy those bind, and the local price falls to **−$11/MWh** (the wind
    bid) in about 3% of hours.
- **Value.**
  - The battery charges at an average of $11.4 and sells at $35.8.
  - $37.6k/month raw, **$40.1k adjusted**. It's the only pick that *gains* on adjusted prices,
    so it doesn't depend on the L4698 question.
- **Congestion relief.** Charging relieves the export constraint: 1,827 MWh, $37.3k. That's
  roughly the wind energy that would otherwise be curtailed, and it makes this the clearest "storage enables
  wind" case.
- **The catch.**
  - Discharge hosting is only ≥ 23 MW in 95% of hours: when the battery discharges, the export
    path is often still full.
  - Grid limits cut the value from $56.8k to $37.6k a month.
  - A smaller battery (50–100 MW), or one paired with a 115 → 230/345 kV upgrade, fits better.
  - A real project would co-locate with the wind farm and earn from avoided curtailment and
    PTCs, which this screen doesn't count.

**Battery sites considered and rejected.**
- **Braidwood (133205).** It would score **$732k/month** on raw prices, by charging at −$74 and
  selling at +$12, but only $25k on adjusted prices. That's entirely the L4698 artefact.
- **Kankakee, Dresden, Elwood, Joliet, Pilot Hill, Kelly Creek.** Same problem: L4698 moves
  their prices by $10–93/MWh. **Don't build on these numbers** until the rating question is
  settled.
- **Sibley/Worthington, MN** (T5529, 27 h): a real pocket but low value ($21.9–25.8k).
- **New Mexico 69 kV** (T7968, L19001): binding, but candidates there are below 115 kV.

## 7. Limitations for merchant investment decisions

This model is a **production-cost-style DC OPF of a synthetic grid**. It is good at ranking
locations by resource quality, grid headroom and where congestion binds. It is not a revenue
forecast. A merchant developer or lender would need to fix or bracket each of the following
before using it to commit capital.

**Prices are too flat, and battery revenue is understated by an order of magnitude or more.**
- **No unit commitment, start-up or no-load costs, minimum generation or ramp limits.** PMIN was
  dropped (LOGBOOK D8) and piecewise costs were averaged into one linear cost per unit (D7).
  Real prices spike when units start, ramp or hit minimums, and fall when inflexible units
  must run. Here nuclear sets the price at night (Aug 22–23 at $11.89) only because it is
  modelled as fully flexible.
- **No scarcity or shortage pricing.**
  - There is no operating reserve demand curve, no reserve shortage prices and no price cap.
    Load is never shed, so the price never leaves the $12–50 range.
  - In 2015, MISO and SPP real-time prices occasionally spiked to hundreds of dollars a MWh.
    In real-time markets those short spikes are a large share of a battery's arbitrage revenue.
  - The model's best result, $0.26/kW-month, should not be compared with real battery
    economics.
- **One deterministic, perfect-foresight, hourly market.**
  - There is no day-ahead/real-time split, no forecast error and no 5-minute volatility.
  - The battery LP knows every price in the month in advance. That *overstates* what is
    captured, but on far too small a spread.
- **Missing revenue streams.**
  - Capacity: MISO's Planning Resource Auction, SPP resource adequacy and accreditation.
  - Ancillary services: regulation, spinning and supplemental reserves. Historically these
    were the largest revenue for batteries.
  - Wind tax credits (PTC/ITC) and REC sales.
  - Bilateral PPAs and hedges.
  - Wind values here are energy-only.

**Price-taker assumption.**
- Each site's value uses base-case prices, as if the new plant didn't change them.
  - A 500 MW wind farm at the edge of the grid lowers its own nodal prices when it runs: the
    cannibalisation effect, which grows as more wind is added.
  - A 200 MW battery in a pocket like Lincoln's flattens the price spread it was built to
    capture, and may remove the congestion altogether.
- The hosting-capacity test is linear and has no redispatch, so it can be optimistic (no
  N-1) or pessimistic (other units would make room).
- **Before any decision, re-solve the month with each candidate plant added** and compare
  revenue. The workflow could do this in about 4.5 h per scenario.

**The network is synthetic and only partially secure.**
- **Midwest24k is a synthetic test case.** The bus names are real substations, but topology,
  impedances and ratings are generated. L4698 shows how one bad rating can dominate the
  whole price picture.
- **No contingency (N-1) constraints.** Real MISO/SPP congestion is mostly on flowgates
  defined by "line A limited after the loss of line B". Base-case thermal limits catch only a
  small part of it. Buffalo Ridge (W3) is the obvious example: congested in practice, open
  here.
- **No losses.** MISO and SPP LMPs include a marginal-loss term that penalises remote
  generation such as W2/W4 and favours generation near load such as W1. A lossless DC model
  misses it.
- **Static ratings, no outages, no planned transmission.** Seasonal and ambient ratings,
  maintenance outages, and transmission built since 2015 (MISO MVP lines, SPP's 345 kV
  expansions) are all absent.
- **No market seams.** MISO, SPP and PJM are modelled as one perfectly coordinated market.
  Northern Illinois is in PJM, and the MISO–SPP seam and interchange limits are ignored.
- **Interconnection is not modelled.**
  - Queue position, study timelines, network upgrade cost allocation and ERIS vs NRIS
    deliverability often decide a project's economics more than the energy price does.
  - The 5% distribution-factor hosting test is a screening proxy only.

**The data describes a single month of 2015.**
- **One month.** August is near the seasonal low for Plains wind and near the peak for load.
  Annual capacity factors, seasonal price shapes and year-to-year weather variation (a
  10–30-year ERA5 record) are needed.
- **2015 fleet and fuel prices.**
  - Coal retirements, new gas, hundreds of GW of new wind, solar and storage in the queues, and
    gas price changes since 2015 change both price levels and congestion patterns.
  - The case also has **no solar in service**. In today's MISO and SPP, solar is reshaping the
    daily price shape that batteries depend on.
- **Simplified inputs.**
  - Hydro is capped at a flat 50% (D15).
  - Load comes from state-level shapes applied to static bus loads (D12), with no load growth,
    DER or data-centre load.
  - Wind uses the nearest ERA5 cell with one turbine model, no wake or availability losses,
    and ERA5's known biases in near-surface wind.

**No project finance.**
- There is no capital cost, O&M, interconnection cost, degradation model (beyond $5/MWh),
  augmentation, taxes, financing, merchant tail or hedge structure, and no NPV/IRR.
- A merchant decision also needs:
  - **basis risk** between the project node and a hub;
  - **congestion hedging** (FTRs/ARRs);
  - **curtailment and negative-price exposure** under contract terms.
  None of these are in the model.

**What the model *is* good for here.**
- A consistent first-pass map of where the resource is, where the 345 kV grid has room, and
  which handful of constraints set local prices.
- Ruling out sites whose apparent value comes from a data error.
- Choosing which candidates deserve a proper add-the-plant re-solve, then a commercial
  production-cost and interconnection study.

## 8. Recommended next steps

1. **Settle the L4698 rating.** Check the source case and the PowerWorld PG for Braidwood. If
   the rating is an error, add a branch-override table to `build_network` and re-solve the
   month (about 4.5 h). Then rerun `line_loading` and `siting`; the rankings will update
   automatically.
2. **Re-solve with each shortlisted plant added.**
   - Wind: add W1–W5 as `p_max_pu`-profiled generators.
   - Batteries: add B1–B5 as `StorageUnit`s with cyclic state of charge per day. This keeps
     days independent, so the parallel structure stays.
   - Compare revenue with the price-taker estimate.
3. **Run more months.** At least one spring and one winter month, so wind capacity factors and
   battery spreads aren't August-only.
4. **Add price formation that batteries depend on.** Reserve requirements with shortage
   pricing, and a minimum-generation/commitment approximation, before reading anything into
   absolute battery revenue.
